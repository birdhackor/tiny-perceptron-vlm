16:                                                                                              Abstract
17:                                                       A very simple way to improve the performance of almost any machine learning
18:                                                       algorithm is to train many different models on the same data and then to average
19:                                                       their predictions [3]. Unfortunately, making predictions using a whole ensemble
20:                                                       of models is cumbersome and may be too computationally expensive to allow de-
21:                                                       ployment to a large number of users, especially if the individual models are large
22:                                                       neural nets. Caruana and his collaborators [1] have shown that it is possible to
23:                                                       compress the knowledge in an ensemble into a single model which is much eas-
24:                                                       ier to deploy and we develop this approach further using a different compression
25:                                                       technique. We achieve some surprising results on MNIST and we show that we
26:                                                       can significantly improve the acoustic model of a heavily used commercial system
27:                                                       by distilling the knowledge in an ensemble of models into a single model. We also
28:                                                       introduce a new type of ensemble composed of one or more full models and many
29:                                                       specialist models which learn to distinguish fine-grained classes that the full mod-
30:                                                       els confuse. Unlike a mixture of experts, these specialist models can be trained
31:                                                       rapidly and in parallel.

34:                                           1 Introduction
35:                                           Many insects have a larval form that is optimized for extracting energy and nutrients from the envi-
36:                                           ronment and a completely different adult form that is optimized for the very different requirements
37:                                           of traveling and reproduction. In large-scale machine learning, we typically use very similar models
38:                                           for the training stage and the deployment stage despite their very different requirements: For tasks
39:                                           like speech and object recognition, training must extract structure from very large, highly redundant
40:                                           datasets but it does not need to operate in real time and it can use a huge amount of computation.
41:                                           Deployment to a large number of users, however, has much more stringent requirements on latency
42:                                           and computational resources. The analogy with insects suggests that we should be willing to train
43:                                           very cumbersome models if that makes it easier to extract structure from the data. The cumbersome
44:                                           model could be an ensemble of separately trained models or a single very large model trained with
45:                                           a very strong regularizer such as dropout [9]. Once the cumbersome model has been trained, we
46:                                           can then use a different kind of training, which we call “distillation” to transfer the knowledge from
47:                                           the cumbersome model to a small model that is more suitable for deployment. A version of this
48:                                           strategy has already been pioneered by Rich Caruana and his collaborators [1]. In their important
49:                                           paper they demonstrate convincingly that the knowledge acquired by a large ensemble of models
50:                                           can be transferred to a single small model.
51:                                           A conceptual block that may have prevented more investigation of this very promising approach is
52:                                           that we tend to identify the knowledge in a trained model with the learned parameter values and this
53:                                           makes it hard to see how we can change the form of the model but keep the same knowledge. A more
54:                                           abstract view of the knowledge, that frees it from any particular instantiation, is that it is a learned

63: mapping from input vectors to output vectors. For cumbersome models that learn to discriminate
64: between a large number of classes, the normal training objective is to maximize the average log
65: probability of the correct answer, but a side-effect of the learning is that the trained model assigns
66: probabilities to all of the incorrect answers and even when these probabilities are very small, some
67: of them are much larger than others. The relative probabilities of incorrect answers tell us a lot about
68: how the cumbersome model tends to generalize. An image of a BMW, for example, may only have
69: a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more
70: probable than mistaking it for a carrot.
71: It is generally accepted that the objective function used for training should reflect the true objective
72: of the user as closely as possible. Despite this, models are usually trained to optimize performance
73: on the training data when the real objective is to generalize well to new data. It would clearly
74: be better to train models to generalize well, but this requires information about the correct way to
75: generalize and this information is not normally available. When we are distilling the knowledge
76: from a large model into a small one, however, we can train the small model to generalize in the same
77: way as the large model. If the cumbersome model generalizes well because, for example, it is the
78: average of a large ensemble of different models, a small model trained to generalize in the same way
79: will typically do much better on test data than a small model that is trained in the normal way on the
80: same training set as was used to train the ensemble.
81: An obvious way to transfer the generalization ability of the cumbersome model to a small model is
82: to use the class probabilities produced by the cumbersome model as “soft targets” for training the
83: small model. For this transfer stage, we could use the same training set or a separate “transfer” set.
84: When the cumbersome model is a large ensemble of simpler models, we can use an arithmetic or
85: geometric mean of their individual predictive distributions as the soft targets. When the soft targets
86: have high entropy, they provide much more information per training case than hard targets and much
87: less variance in the gradient between training cases, so the small model can often be trained on much
88: less data than the original cumbersome model and using a much higher learning rate.
89: For tasks like MNIST in which the cumbersome model almost always produces the correct answer
90: with very high confidence, much of the information about the learned function resides in the ratios
91: of very small probabilities in the soft targets. For example, one version of a 2 may be given a
92: probability of 10−6 of being a 3 and 10−9 of being a 7 whereas for another version it may be the
93: other way around. This is valuable information that defines a rich similarity structure over the data
94: (i. e. it says which 2’s look like 3’s and which look like 7’s) but it has very little influence on the
95: cross-entropy cost function during the transfer stage because the probabilities are so close to zero.
96: Caruana and his collaborators circumvent this problem by using the logits (the inputs to the final
97: softmax) rather than the probabilities produced by the softmax as the targets for learning the small
98: model and they minimize the squared difference between the logits produced by the cumbersome
99: model and the logits produced by the small model. Our more general solution, called “distillation”,
100: is to raise the temperature of the final softmax until the cumbersome model produces a suitably soft
101: set of targets. We then use the same high temperature when training the small model to match these
102: soft targets. We show later that matching the logits of the cumbersome model is actually a special
103: case of distillation.
104: The transfer set that is used to train the small model could consist entirely of unlabeled data [1]
105: or we could use the original training set. We have found that using the original training set works
106: well, especially if we add a small term to the objective function that encourages the small model
107: to predict the true targets as well as matching the soft targets provided by the cumbersome model.
108: Typically, the small model cannot exactly match the soft targets and erring in the direction of the
109: correct answer turns out to be helpful.

126: In the simplest form of distillation, knowledge is transferred to the distilled model by training it on
127: a transfer set and using a soft target distribution for each case in the transfer set that is produced by
128: using the cumbersome model with a high temperature in its softmax. The same high temperature is
129: used when training the distilled model, but after it has been trained it uses a temperature of 1.
130: When the correct labels are known for all or some of the transfer set, this method can be significantly
131: improved by also training the distilled model to produce the correct labels. One way to do this is
132: to use the correct labels to modify the soft targets, but we found that a better way is to simply use
133: a weighted average of two different objective functions. The first objective function is the cross
134: entropy with the soft targets and this cross entropy is computed using the same high temperature in
135: the softmax of the distilled model as was used for generating the soft targets from the cumbersome
136: model. The second objective function is the cross entropy with the correct labels. This is computed
137: using exactly the same logits in softmax of the distilled model but at a temperature of 1. We found
138: that the best results were generally obtained by using a condiderably lower weight on the second
139: objective function. Since the magnitudes of the gradients produced by the soft targets scale as 1/T 2
140: it is important to multiply them by T 2 when using both hard and soft targets. This ensures that the
141: relative contributions of the hard and soft targets remain roughly unchanged if the temperature used
142: for distillation is changed while experimenting with meta-parameters.
