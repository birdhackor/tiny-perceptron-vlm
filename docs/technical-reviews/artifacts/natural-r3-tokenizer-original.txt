82: So if simple space and punctuation tokenization is unsatisfactory, why not simply tokenize on characters?
83: 
84: <Youtube id="ssLq_EK2jLE"/>
85: 
86: While character tokenization is very simple and would greatly reduce memory and time complexity it makes it much harder
87: for the model to learn meaningful input representations. *E.g.* learning a meaningful context-independent
88: representation for the letter `"t"` is much harder than learning a context-independent representation for the word
89: `"today"`. Therefore, character tokenization is often accompanied by a loss of performance. So to get the best of
90: both worlds, transformers models use a hybrid between word-level and character-level tokenization called **subword**
91: tokenization.
92: 
93: ## Subword tokenization
94: 
95: <Youtube id="zHvTiHr506c"/>
96: 
97: Subword tokenization algorithms rely on the principle that frequently used words should not be split into smaller
98: subwords, but rare words should be decomposed into meaningful subwords. For instance `"annoyingly"` might be
99: considered a rare word and could be decomposed into `"annoying"` and `"ly"`. Both `"annoying"` and `"ly"` as
100: stand-alone subwords would appear more frequently while at the same time the meaning of `"annoyingly"` is kept by the
101: composite meaning of `"annoying"` and `"ly"`. This is especially useful in agglutinative languages such as Turkish,
102: where you can form (almost) arbitrarily long complex words by stringing together subwords.
103: 
104: Subword tokenization allows the model to have a reasonable vocabulary size while being able to learn meaningful
105: context-independent representations. In addition, subword tokenization enables the model to process words it has never
106: seen before, by decomposing them into known subwords. For instance, the [`~transformers.BertTokenizer`] tokenizes
