L23:                                                                                       Abstract
L24:                                                    Making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following
L25:                                                    a user’s intent. For example, large language models can generate outputs that
L26:                                                    are untruthful, toxic, or simply not helpful to the user. In other words, these
L27:                                                    models are not aligned with their users. In this paper, we show an avenue for
L28:                                                    aligning language models with user intent on a wide range of tasks by fine-tuning
L29:                                                    with human feedback. Starting with a set of labeler-written prompts and prompts
L30:                                                    submitted through the OpenAI API, we collect a dataset of labeler demonstrations
L31:                                                    of the desired model behavior, which we use to fine-tune GPT-3 using supervised
L32:                                                    learning. We then collect a dataset of rankings of model outputs, which we use to
L33:                                                    further fine-tune this supervised model using reinforcement learning from human
L34:                                                    feedback. We call the resulting models InstructGPT. In human evaluations on
L35:                                                    our prompt distribution, outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are
L36:                                                    preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters.
L37:                                                    Moreover, InstructGPT models show improvements in truthfulness and reductions
L38:                                                    in toxic output generation while having minimal performance regressions on public
L39:                                                    NLP datasets. Even though InstructGPT still makes simple mistakes, our results
L40:                                                    show that fine-tuning with human feedback is a promising direction for aligning
L41:                                                    language models with human intent.

L294: 3         Methods and experimental details
L295: 3.1       High-level methodology
L296: 
L297: Our methodology follows that of Ziegler et al. (2019) and Stiennon et al. (2020), who applied
L298: it in the stylistic continuation and summarization domains. We start with a pretrained language
L299: model (Radford et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2021; Rae et al., 2021; Thoppilan et al.,
L300: 2022), a distribution of prompts on which we want our model to produce aligned outputs, and a team
L301: of trained human labelers (see Sections 3.4 for details). We then apply the following three steps
L302: (Figure 2).
L303: 
L304: Step 1: Collect demonstration data, and train a supervised policy. Our labelers provide demon-
L305: strations of the desired behavior on the input prompt distribution (see Section 3.2 for details on this
L306: distribution). We then fine-tune a pretrained GPT-3 model on this data using supervised learning.
L307: 
L308: Step 2: Collect comparison data, and train a reward model. We collect a dataset of comparisons
L309: between model outputs, where labelers indicate which output they prefer for a given input. We then
L310: train a reward model to predict the human-preferred output.
L311: 
L312: Step 3: Optimize a policy against the reward model using PPO. We use the output of the
L313: RM as a scalar reward. We fine-tune the supervised policy to optimize this reward using the PPO
L314: algorithm (Schulman et al., 2017).
L315: Steps 2 and 3 can be iterated continuously; more comparison data is collected on the current best
L316: policy, which is used to train a new RM and then a new policy. In practice, most of our comparison
L317: data comes from our supervised policies, with some coming from our PPO policies.

L411: 3.5       Models
L412: 
L413: We start with the GPT-3 pretrained language models from Brown et al. (2020). These models are
L414: trained on a broad distribution of Internet data and are adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks,
L415: but have poorly characterized behavior. Starting from these models, we then train models with three
L416: different techniques:
L417: 
L418: Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). We fine-tune GPT-3 on our labeler demonstrations using supervised
L419: learning. We trained for 16 epochs, using a cosine learning rate decay, and residual dropout of 0.2.
L420: We do our final SFT model selection based on the RM score on the validation set. Similarly to Wu
L421: et al. (2021), we find that our SFT models overfit on validation loss after 1 epoch; however, we find
L422: that training for more epochs helps both the RM score and human preference ratings, despite this
L423: overfitting.
L424: 
L425: Reward modeling (RM). Starting from the SFT model with the final unembedding layer removed,
L426: we trained a model to take in a prompt and response, and output a scalar reward. In this paper we
L427: only use 6B RMs, as this saves a lot of compute, and we found that 175B RM training could be
L428: unstable and thus was less suitable to be used as the value function during RL (see Appendix C for
L429: more details).
L430: In Stiennon et al. (2020), the RM is trained on a dataset of comparisons between two model outputs
L431: on the same input. They use a cross-entropy loss, with the comparisons as labels—the difference in
L432: rewards represents the log odds that one response will be preferred to the other by a human labeler.

L434: K = 9 responses to rank. This produces K  2 comparisons for each prompt shown to a labeler. Since
L435: comparisons are very correlated within each labeling task, we found that if we simply shuffle the

L481: Finally, since the RM loss is invariant to shifts in reward, we normalize the reward model using a bias
L482: so that the labeler demonstrations achieve a mean score of 0 before doing RL.
L483: 
L484: Reinforcement learning (RL). Once again following Stiennon et al. (2020), we fine-tuned the
L485: SFT model on our environment using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). The environment is a bandit
L486: environment which presents a random customer prompt and expects a response to the prompt. Given
L487: the prompt and response, it produces a reward determined by the reward model and ends the episode.
L488: In addition, we add a per-token KL penalty from the SFT model at each token to mitigate over-
L489: optimization of the reward model. The value function is initialized from the RM. We call these
L490: models “PPO.”
L491: We also experiment with mixing the pretraining gradients into the PPO gradients, in order to fix the
L492: performance regressions on public NLP datasets. We call these models “PPO-ptx.” We maximize the
L493: following combined objective function in RL training:
