L10:                                                                                            Abstract
L11:                                                      We propose a new family of policy gradient methods for reinforcement learning, which al-
L12:                                                  ternate between sampling data through interaction with the environment, and optimizing a
L13:                                                  “surrogate” objective function using stochastic gradient ascent. Whereas standard policy gra-
L14:                                                  dient methods perform one gradient update per data sample, we propose a novel objective
L15:                                                  function that enables multiple epochs of minibatch updates. The new methods, which we call
L16:                                                  proximal policy optimization (PPO), have some of the benefits of trust region policy optimiza-
L17:                                                  tion (TRPO), but they are much simpler to implement, more general, and have better sample
L18:                                                  complexity (empirically). Our experiments test PPO on a collection of benchmark tasks, includ-
L19:                                                  ing simulated robotic locomotion and Atari game playing, and we show that PPO outperforms
L20:                                                  other online policy gradient methods, and overall strikes a favorable balance between sample
L21:                                                  complexity, simplicity, and wall-time.

L24:                                          1       Introduction
L25:                                          In recent years, several different approaches have been proposed for reinforcement learning with
L26:                                          neural network function approximators. The leading contenders are deep Q-learning [Mni+15],
L27:                                          “vanilla” policy gradient methods [Mni+16], and trust region / natural policy gradient methods
L28:                                          [Sch+15b]. However, there is room for improvement in developing a method that is scalable (to
L29:                                          large models and parallel implementations), data efficient, and robust (i.e., successful on a variety
L30:                                          of problems without hyperparameter tuning). Q-learning (with function approximation) fails on
L31:                                          many simple problems1 and is poorly understood, vanilla policy gradient methods have poor data
L32:                                          effiency and robustness; and trust region policy optimization (TRPO) is relatively complicated,
L33:                                          and is not compatible with architectures that include noise (such as dropout) or parameter sharing
L34:                                          (between the policy and value function, or with auxiliary tasks).
L35:                                               This paper seeks to improve the current state of affairs by introducing an algorithm that attains
L36:                                          the data efficiency and reliable performance of TRPO, while using only first-order optimization.
L37:                                          We propose a novel objective with clipped probability ratios, which forms a pessimistic estimate
L38:                                          (i.e., lower bound) of the performance of the policy. To optimize policies, we alternate between
L39:                                          sampling data from the policy and performing several epochs of optimization on the sampled data.
L40:                                               Our experiments compare the performance of various different versions of the surrogate objec-
L41:                                          tive, and find that the version with the clipped probability ratios performs best. We also compare
L42:                                          PPO to several previous algorithms from the literature. On continuous control tasks, it performs
L43:                                          better than the algorithms we compare against. On Atari, it performs significantly better (in terms
L44:                                          of sample complexity) than A2C and similarly to ACER though it is much simpler.
L45:                                              1
L46:                                              While DQN works well on game environments like the Arcade Learning Environment [Bel+15] with discrete
L47:                                          action spaces, it has not been demonstrated to perform well on continuous control benchmarks such as those in
L48:                                          OpenAI Gym [Bro+16] and described by Duan et al. [Dua+16].

L54: 2.1   Policy Gradient Methods
L55: Policy gradient methods work by computing an estimator of the policy gradient and plugging it
L56: into a stochastic gradient ascent algorithm. The most commonly used gradient estimator has the
L57: form

L62: Here, the expectation Êt [. . .] indicates the empirical average over a finite batch of samples, in an
L63: algorithm that alternates between sampling and optimization. Implementations that use automatic
L64: differentiation software work by constructing an objective function whose gradient is the policy
L65: gradient estimator; the estimator ĝ is obtained by differentiating the objective
L66:                                                     h                    i
L67:                                       LP G (θ) = Êt log πθ (at | st )Ât .                         (2)

L74: 2.2   Trust Region Methods
L75: In TRPO [Sch+15b], an objective function (the “surrogate” objective) is maximized subject to a
L76: constraint on the size of the policy update. Specifically,
L77:                                                                 
L78:                                              πθ (at | st )
L79:                             maximize Êt                     Ât                           (3)
L80:                                 θ           πθold (at | st )
L81:                             subject to Êt [KL[πθold (· | st ), πθ (· | st )]] ≤ δ.                 (4)
L82: 
L83: Here, θold is the vector of policy parameters before the update. This problem can efficiently be
L84: approximately solved using the conjugate gradient algorithm, after making a linear approximation
L85: to the objective and a quadratic approximation to the constraint.
L86:     The theory justifying TRPO actually suggests using a penalty instead of a constraint, i.e.,
L87: solving the unconstrained optimization problem
L88:                                                                                              
L89:                                      πθ (at | st )
L90:                      maximize Êt                    Ât − β KL[πθold (· | st ), πθ (· | st )]  (5)
L91:                          θ          πθold (at | st )
L92: 
L93: for some coefficient β. This follows from the fact that a certain surrogate objective (which computes
L94: the max KL over states instead of the mean) forms a lower bound (i.e., a pessimistic bound) on the
L95: performance of the policy π. TRPO uses a hard constraint rather than a penalty because it is hard
L96: to choose a single value of β that performs well across different problems—or even within a single
L97: problem, where the the characteristics change over the course of learning. Hence, to achieve our goal
L98: of a first-order algorithm that emulates the monotonic improvement of TRPO, experiments show
L99: that it is not sufficient to simply choose a fixed penalty coefficient β and optimize the penalized
L100: objective Equation (5) with SGD; additional modifications are required.

L175: 4    Adaptive KL Penalty Coefficient
L176: Another approach, which can be used as an alternative to the clipped surrogate objective, or in
L177: addition to it, is to use a penalty on KL divergence, and to adapt the penalty coefficient so that we
L178: achieve some target value of the KL divergence dtarg each policy update. In our experiments, we
L179: found that the KL penalty performed worse than the clipped surrogate objective, however, we’ve
L180: included it here because it’s an important baseline.
L181:     In the simplest instantiation of this algorithm, we perform the following steps in each policy
L182: update:
L183:     • Using several epochs of minibatch SGD, optimize the KL-penalized objective
L184:                                                                                                    
L185:                       KLP EN               πθ (at | st )
L186:                      L        (θ) = Êt                    Ât − β KL[πθold (· | st ), πθ (· | st )]                 (8)
L187:                                           πθold (at | st )
L188: 
L189:     • Compute d = Êt [KL[πθold (· | st ), πθ (· | st )]]
L190:          – If d < dtarg /1.5, β ← β/2
L191:          – If d > dtarg × 1.5, β ← β × 2
L192: The updated β is used for the next policy update. With this scheme, we occasionally see policy
L193: updates where the KL divergence is significantly different from dtarg , however, these are rare, and
L194: β quickly adjusts. The parameters 1.5 and 2 above are chosen heuristically, but the algorithm is
L195: not very sensitive to them. The initial value of β is a another hyperparameter but is not important
L196: in practice because the algorithm quickly adjusts it.

L200: The surrogate losses from the previous sections can be computed and differentiated with a minor
L201: change to a typical policy gradient implementation. For implementations that use automatic dif-
L202: ferentation, one simply constructs the loss LCLIP or LKLP EN instead of LP G , and one performs
L203: multiple steps of stochastic gradient ascent on this objective.

L205: learned state-value function V (s); for example, generalized advantage estimation [Sch+15a], or the

L211: an entropy bonus to ensure sufficient exploration, as suggested in past work [Wil92; Mni+16].
