Read on 2026-10-04. Full third-party downloads remain in ignored `outputs/natural-v4/factual-research/12.1/`; the accompanying retrieval receipt records original URLs, final URLs, retrieval time, and SHA-256. The notes below are my own summaries.

- OpenStax, *University Physics Volume 1*, published 2016-09-19, current HTML retrieved 2026-10-04: §17.1, opening pressure/compression/rarefaction explanation and “Models Describing Sound,” equation 17.1. The pressure fluctuation is signed relative to an average pressure, and a sinusoidal model has separate maximum pressure excursion, angular frequency, and initial phase. This supports the lesson’s air-pressure and signed-waveform intuition; a generated float value is not a calibrated pressure reading.
- OpenStax §16.2, “Modeling a One-Dimensional Sinusoidal Wave using a Wave Function,” equations 16.4–16.5 and “Finding the Characteristics of a Sinusoidal Wave.” The sine ranges between ±1 and repeats after 2π; multiplication sets its amplitude, while frequency is the inverse period. Phase is the sine argument, with an independent initial phase. The lesson’s 0-to-1 cycle progress is normalized phase, with angle 2π times the progress; it is not the same as vertical amplitude. In the lesson’s zero-initial-phase generator, zero on the upward and downward crossings denotes different phases.
- OpenStax §17.3, equation 17.11 and “Hearing and Pitch,” including the equal-loudness discussion/figure 17.15. Physical intensity depends on pressure amplitude squared for the stated medium; perceived pitch relates to frequency, and perceived loudness depends on both intensity and frequency. The textbook also describes instrument timbre through multiple frequency/intensity components. These conditions support the lesson’s separation of amplitude, frequency, and perceptual loudness, without assigning a fixed acoustic decibel value to 0.5.
- Adobe Audition original manual, “Digitizing audio,” last updated 2021-04-27: “Analog audio: positive and negative voltage,” “Digital audio: zeroes and ones,” “Understanding sample rate,” “Measuring amplitude in dBFS,” and “Audio file contents and size.” Samples represent ordered snapshots, sample rate states their count per second, a header stores the rate, and stereo has two channels. The manual explicitly separates digital dBFS from original acoustic sound-pressure level. Only these topics support this review; its informal Nyquist boundary/reconstruction wording is not used as evidence for a strict sampling theorem.

Independent derivation: for index n, t=n/16000 seconds and y=0.5 sin(2π f n/16000). N=round(0.1×16000)=1600, so indices are 0…1599, nominal duration N/16000=0.1 s, and the last timestamp is 0.0999375 s. At 440 Hz, index 1 has cycle progress 440/16000=0.0275 and scalar amplitude 0.08596455013970478; index 2 is 0.1693689601226457. At 880 Hz these become 0.055, 0.1693689601226457, and 0.3187119948743449. The two nominal windows contain 44 and 88 cycles. These frequencies are far below the 8 kHz sampling boundary; the statement is about this example, not arbitrarily high-frequency sampled tones. At cycle fractions 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1, the ideal amplitudes are 0, +0.5, 0, −0.5, 0. Floating sine evaluation has tiny residuals at theoretical zeros.
