21:                                                                                               Abstract
22:                                                      Evaluating large language model (LLM) based chat assistants is challenging due to
23:                                                      their broad capabilities and the inadequacy of existing benchmarks in measuring
24:                                                      human preferences. To address this, we explore using strong LLMs as judges to
25:                                                      evaluate these models on more open-ended questions. We examine the usage and
26:                                                      limitations of LLM-as-a-judge, such as position and verbosity biases and limited
27:                                                      reasoning ability, and propose solutions to migrate some of them. We then verify
28:                                                      the agreement between LLM judges and human preferences by introducing two
29:                                                      benchmarks: MT-bench, a multi-turn question set; and Chatbot Arena, a crowd-
30:                                                      sourced battle platform. Our results reveal that strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can
31:                                                      match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over
32:                                                      80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans. Hence, LLM-as-a-
33:                                                      judge is a scalable and explainable way to approximate human preferences, which
34:                                                      are otherwise very expensive to obtain. Additionally, we show our benchmark and
35:                                                      traditional benchmarks complement each other by evaluating several variants of
36:                                                      LLaMA/Vicuna. We will publicly release 80 MT-bench questions, 3K expert votes,
37:                                                      and 30K conversations with human preferences from Chatbot Arena 2 .

289: Verbosity bias is when an LLM judge favors longer, verbose responses, even if they are not as clear,
290: high-quality, or accurate as shorter alternatives.
291: To examine this bias, we design a “repetitive list” attack with model answers from MT-bench. We
292: first select 23 model answers from MT-bench that contain a numbered list. We then make them
293: unnecessarily verbose by asking GPT-4 to rephrase the list without adding any new information and
294: insert the rephrased new list to the beginning of the original list. For example, if the original response
295: contains 5 items, then the new response will contain 10 items but the first 5 items are rephrased from
296: the original 5 items. An example is shown in Figure 11 (Appendix). We define the attack is successful
297: if an LLM judge thinks the new response is better than the old response. Table 3 shows the failure
298: rate of LLM judges under this attack, demonstrating that all LLMs may be prone to verbosity bias
299: though GPT-4 defends significantly better than others. As a calibration, we find LLM judges are
300: able to correctly judge identical answers (i.e., they always return a tie for two identical answers) but
301: cannot pass the more advanced “repetitive list” attack.
302: Self-enhancement bias. We adopt the term “self-enhancement bias” from social cognition litera-
303: ture [4] to describe the effect that LLM judges may favor the answers generated by themselves.
304: We examine this effect statistically. Figure 3(b) shows the win rate (w/o tie) of six models under
305: different LLM judges and humans. Compared to humans, we do observe that some judges favor
306: certain models. For example, GPT-4 favors itself with a 10% higher win rate; Claude-v1 favors itself
307: with a 25% higher win rate. However, they also favor other models and GPT-3.5 does not favor itself.
308: Due to limited data and small differences, our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit
309: a self-enhancement bias. Conducting a controlled study is challenging because we cannot easily
310: rephrase a response to fit the style of another model without changing the quality.

327: 3.4   Addressing limitations
328: 
329: We present a few methods to address position bias and the limited grading ability for math questions.
330: Swapping positions. The position bias can be addressed by simple solutions. A conservative
331: approach is to call a judge twice by swapping the order of two answers and only declare a win when
332: an answer is preferred in both orders. If the results are inconsistent after swapping, we can call it a
333: tie. Another more aggressive approach is to assign positions randomly, which can be effective at a
334: large scale with the correct expectations. In the following experiments, we use the conservative one.

375: 4           Agreement Evaluation
376: 
377: We study the agreement between different LLM judges and humans on MT-bench and Chatbot
378: Arena datasets. On MT-bench, we also study the agreement among humans. MT-bench represents a
379: small-scale study with controlled human evaluation, while Chatbot Arena represents a larger-scale
380: study with crowdsourced human evaluation in the wild.
381: 
382: 
383: 4.1           Setup
384: 
385: MT-bench We generate answers for all 80 questions with 6 models: GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-V1,
386: Vicuna-13B, Alpaca-13B [33], and LLaMA-13B [34]. We then use 2 kinds of judges: LLM judges
387: and 50 expert-level human labelers. The labelers are mostly graduate students so they are considered
388: experts and more skilled than average crowd workers. We let LLM judges evaluate all pairs and let
389: each human evaluate at least 20 random pairs. The detailed data collection process is in Appendix C.
390: Chatbot Arena We randomly sample 3K single-turn votes from 30K arena data, which covers models
391: including GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude, Vicuna-7B/13B, Koala-13B [13], Alpaca-13B, LLaMA-13B, and
392: Dolly-12B. We use two kinds of judges: LLM judges and collected crowd judges (2114 unique IPs).
393: Metrics We define the agreement between two types of judges as the probability of randomly selected
394: individuals (but not identical) of each type agreeing on a randomly selected question. See more
395: explanation in Appendix D.3. Average win rate is the average of win rates against all other players.
396: These metrics can be computed with or without including tie votes.
